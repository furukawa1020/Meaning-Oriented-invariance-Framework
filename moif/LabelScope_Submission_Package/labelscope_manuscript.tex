\title{LabelScope: A Claim-Capping Framework for Auditing Operational Labels in Physiology-Based Human-Machine Systems}

\author{Kotaro Furukawa}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Physiology-based human-machine systems (HMS) often train predictive models on operational labels such as questionnaire scores, task conditions, behavioral errors, or affective annotations. However, high predictive accuracy does not by itself establish that the target label can support the HMS claim made from the model. A label may be temporally sparse, behaviorally entangled, or structurally unsupported in physiological feature space. In such cases, model performance may reflect the learnability of an operational proxy rather than evidence for a physiology-grounded user-state claim. This paper introduces LabelScope, a conservative pre-modeling audit framework for claim-relative state-label validity in physiology-based HMS. LabelScope audits whether an operational label can support a specified HMS claim under a given modeling resolution, proxy interpretation, feature representation, and deployment context. The framework consists of Resolution, Proxy, and Structure audit modules, and assigns a capped claim level $L_{Final} \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ using a conservative claim-capping rule. The case studies on the WESAD and SWELL-KW datasets illustrate how LabelScope can cap overbroad state claims under specified audit assumptions. By detecting failure modes such as behavioral-proxy risk and pseudo-replication, LabelScope grounds the system's action policy in audited evidence, supporting the interpretation of predictive accuracy within defensible claim boundaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{P}{hysiological} sensing is increasingly integrated into human-machine systems (HMS) to infer operator state without requiring explicit input. By monitoring signals such as heart rate variability (HRV), electrodermal activity (EDA), and electroencephalography (EEG), researchers aim to build systems that can adapt to a user's stress, fatigue, or cognitive load \cite{picard1997,martinez2017}. These systems, often positioned under affective computing or cognitive load monitoring, have been applied across domains including healthcare, industrial safety, and assistive technology \cite{parasuraman2000}.

Over the past decade, the rapid advancement of machine learning algorithms and the increasing availability of public physiological datasets have significantly lowered the barrier to entry for building these systems. Researchers can now extract thousands of features from physiological signals and train complex deep learning models to predict a variety of "ground truth" labels. However, as the field has matured, a critical methodological gap has emerged: the tendency to equate predictive accuracy with the validity of the underlying user-state claim. In many HMS studies, a high-accuracy model trained on an operational label---such as a questionnaire score or a specific task condition---is frequently treated as definitive evidence that the system has successfully "recognized" or "detected" the intended psychological state \cite{Calvo2010}. 

We argue that this focus on predictive accuracy creates a \textbf{methodological risk} in the interpretation of results. High accuracy measures only the learnability of an operational target within a specific dataset and feature representation. It does not, by itself, justify the scope of the HMS claim made from that model. The central problem addressed in this paper is that operational labels in physiology-based HMS are often underdetermined by the physiological evidence. A model may achieve high accuracy by exploiting behavioral-proxy risk, pseudo-replication artifacts, or dataset-specific noise, rather than by capturing a robust physiological signature of the intended psychological state. 

This underdetermination has direct consequences for HMS reliability. In a closed-loop HMS, the system's action policy is coupled to the inferred user state. If a system triggers an intervention based on an entangled label---for example, an adaptive learning environment incorrectly lowering task difficulty because it conflated a student's physical posture shift (a Level 2 behavioral proxy) for cognitive overload (a Level 3 internal state)---the resulting action disrupts the user's workflow and may erode trust in system autonomy \cite{parasuraman2000}. To prevent such failures, state-label validity must be treated as a systematic pre-modeling audit discipline before predictive performance is allowed to govern system behavior.

This paper introduces \textbf{LabelScope}, a systematic claim-relative audit framework that evaluates whether a physiological operational label can support an intended HMS state claim. We demonstrate the framework through retrospective audit case studies on the WESAD and SWELL-KW datasets. LabelScope detects failure modes such as behavioral-proxy risk and pseudo-replication, assigning a conservative claim cap that grounds the system's action policy. These case studies illustrate how LabelScope can cap overbroad state claims under specified audit assumptions, supporting the interpretation of predictive accuracy within defensible physiological boundaries.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Physiological HMS and the Affective Computing Heritage}
Physiology-based HMS are deeply rooted in the foundational goals of affective computing. Since Picard's seminal work in 1997 \cite{picard1997}, the field has sought to build computational systems that can relate to, arise from, or influence human emotions and internal states. This heritage has provided the technical scaffolding for modern HMS, including signal processing techniques for heart rate variability (HRV), skin conductance \cite{boucsein2012}, and brain-computer interfaces. Over the past two decades, the focus has shifted from basic emotion recognition to the sensing of complex states such as cognitive load and stress. 

Surveys of the field, such as those by Calvo and D'Mello \cite{Calvo2010} and Bota et al. \cite{Bota2019}, highlight the increasing sophistication of physiological sensing pipelines. However, as sensor technology and modeling techniques have advanced, a new phase of methodological refinement has become necessary. While initial studies established the foundational links between physiology and psychology, the deployment of these models in adaptive HMS requires a more granular understanding of state-label supportability. The challenge of establishing a ``ground truth'' for unobservable states remains a central methodological question, one that now requires explicit auditing of the interpretive scope of our models.

\subsection{Adaptive Automation and Human-Machine Teaming}
The ultimate goal of physiological HMS is to enable adaptive automation, where the system dynamically adjusts its intervention (e.g., task offloading, alert suppression) based on the operator's cognitive or affective state \cite{parasuraman2000}. A critical challenge in human-machine teaming is trust calibration \cite{lee2004}. When an adaptive system intervenes based on a spurious state inference, it degrades the operator's trust and situational awareness \cite{wickens2008}. LabelScope addresses this by providing a pre-intervention audit, ensuring that the system's action policy is grounded in a defensible physiological claim rather than an unverified operational proxy.

\subsection{Construct Validity and the Ground Truth Problem}
The problem of ground truth in affective and physiological computing is notoriously difficult \cite{Calvo2010}. Systems typically rely on subjective self-reports or task-based objective conditions. This challenge relates to the broader theory of Construct Validity \cite{messick1989}, which emphasizes that validity is a property of the \textit{interpretations} of scores, not the scores themselves. LabelScope does not replace construct validation; it provides computable audit checks for whether an operational label can support a specified HMS claim under a given modeling setup. We treat the operational label as a weak or proxy label \cite{zhou2018} that must be audited for its supportability at a specific resolution before state claims are made.

Recent literature has highlighted severe methodological flaws in physiological ML, particularly data leakage through pseudo-replication (where overlapping windows from the same label observation inflate accuracy) \cite{pseudo2015} and behavioral-proxy risk (where physiological sensors act as high-variance motion detectors) \cite{chiossi2024}. 

While existing reporting frameworks such as Model Cards \cite{mitchell2019} and Datasheets \cite{gebru2021} document dataset and model properties, they do not prescribe an algorithmic bound on the physiological claim that can be supported by a given label. Table \ref{tab:framework_comparison} summarizes the distinction between LabelScope and these established frameworks. LabelScope addresses this gap by integrating known failure modes into a rule-based audit pipeline that outputs a \textbf{claim cap}---a computable ceiling on the HMS claim supported by the tested label, features, and covariates.

\subsection{Mathematical Formalization}
Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ be a physiological feature matrix derived exclusively from physiological modalities. Let $y$ be an operational label. Let $C_B \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ be behavioral covariates. LabelScope evaluates the tuple $(X, y, C_B, S)$ and returns $L_{Final} = \min(L_{RA}, L_{PA}, L_{SA})$.

\subsection{Audit Module 1: Resolution Audit (RA)}
RA addresses the risk of \textbf{pseudo-replication} \cite{pseudo2015}. We define the replication ratio $R_P = N_{samples}/N_{independent\_obs}$. If $R_P > \tau_{RA}$, the claim is capped at Level 0 (Unsupported Resolution).

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Resolution Audit (RA)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $N_{samples}, N_{ind\_obs}, \tau_{RA}$
\STATE $R_P \gets N_{samples} / N_{ind\_obs}$
\STATE $L_{RA} \gets (R_P > \tau_{RA}) ? 0 : 3$
\RETURN $L_{RA}, R_P$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Audit Module 2: Proxy Audit (PA)}
Proxy Audit (PA) detects \textbf{behavioral-proxy risk}. Physiological signals are highly sensitive to physical activity; if an operational label is strongly correlated with behavioral covariates (e.g., posture shifts or task actions), the system may be acting as a behavioral proxy detector rather than an internal-state monitor. We define the subject-wise proxy index $\rho_{LC,s}$ for subject $s$ as:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{LC,s} = \max_{k \in \{1 \dots K\}} |\mathrm{Spearman}(y_s, c_{s,k})|
\end{equation}
where $y_s$ is the label vector and $\{c_{s,k}\}$ are observed behavioral covariates for subject $s$. The group-level index $\rho_{LC}$ is calculated as the mean of subject-wise indices. If $\rho_{LC}$ exceeds the threshold $\tau_{PA}$, the claim is capped at Level 2. Critically, passing the PA only clears the system of \textit{observed} covariates; it cannot guarantee the absence of unobserved behavioral or environmental entanglements.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Proxy Audit (PA)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $\{y_s\}, \{c_{s,k}\}, \tau_{PA}$
\FOR{each subject $s$}
    \STATE $\rho_{LC,s} \gets \max_k |\text{Spearman}(y_s, c_{s,k})|$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\rho_{LC} \gets \frac{1}{S}\sum_s \rho_{LC,s}$
\STATE $L_{PA} \gets 2$ if $\rho_{LC} > \tau_{PA}$ else $3$
\RETURN $L_{PA}, \rho_{LC}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Audit Module 3: Structure Audit (SA)}
Structure Audit (SA) evaluates neighborhood coherence $T_{obs}$ in the physiological feature space $X$, which must be disjoint from $C_B$. We define $T_{obs}$ as the $k$-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) label coherence:
\begin{equation}
T_{obs} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\left(y_i = \text{mode}(\{y_j\}_{j \in \mathcal{N}_k(x_i)})\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}_k(x_i)$ is the set of $k$ nearest neighbors of $x_i$ using Euclidean distance after subject-wise z-score normalization. To account for temporal autocorrelation, the permutation test uses \textbf{block-shuffling}. The label time-series is divided into contiguous task blocks of length $L$, which are permuted within each subject before calculating the surrogate coherence $T_{shuf}^{(b)}$. The empirical p-value is calculated over $B$ permutations. If $\hat{p}_{shuf} \ge \alpha$, the claim is capped at Level 1.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Structure Audit (SA)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $X, y, B, \alpha$
\STATE $T_{obs} \gets \text{calc\_coherence}(X, y)$
\FOR{$b = 1$ \TO $B$}
    \STATE $y_{shuf} \gets \text{block\_shuffle}(y)$
    \STATE $T_{shuf}^{(b)} \gets \text{calc\_coherence}(X, y_{shuf})$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\hat{p}_{shuf} \gets (1 + \sum [T_{shuf} \ge T_{obs}]) / (B+1)$
\STATE $L_{SA} \gets (\hat{p}_{shuf} \ge \alpha) ? 1 : 3$
\RETURN $L_{SA}, \hat{p}_{shuf}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Claim Level Assignment}
The final claim level is determined by the most restrictive constraint:
\begin{equation}
    L_{Final} = \min(L_{RA},\; L_{PA},\; L_{SA})
\end{equation}

\subsection{SWELL-KW Case Study: Redesigning Label Resolution}
The initial minute-level claim on SWELL-KW resulted in $R_P = 15$, capping the claim at \textbf{Level 0}. By redesigning the modeling resolution to the 15-minute block level, $R_P$ is reduced to 1. This removes the RA-imposed Level 0 cap only; PA and SA must be re-run under the redesigned claim specification before assigning a final claim level.

LabelScope requires researchers to report their predictive performance \textit{within the context of an audited claim level}. This ensures that engineering convenience (e.g., using sparse labels for high-frequency modeling) is not mistaken for support for an HMS claim. We advocate that future HMS research report claim supportability levels alongside traditional performance metrics.

\subsection{LabelScope as an Engineering Safeguard}
Beyond its role as a reporting discipline, LabelScope serves as an engineering safeguard during the HMS design process. By identifying failure modes such as resolution mismatch or behavioral-proxy risk \textit{before} the system is deployed, designers can make informed decisions to improve the system's reliability.
For example, if a ``stress monitor'' is capped at Level 2 due to movement-proxy risk, the designer can either:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Refine the Hardware}: Add additional sensors to explicitly control for behavioral covariates.
    \item \textbf{Refine the Label}: Collect higher-resolution labels that are less entangled with activity.
    \item \textbf{Refine the Claim}: Explicitly report the system as a ``task-behavior monitor'' rather than a pure ``physiological state monitor.''
\end{enumerate}
In adaptive systems, the audited level can be used as a guard condition for action policies. Table \ref{tab:action_policy} details how each LabelScope level maps to permitted and prohibited system interventions.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
While LabelScope provides a rigorous foundation, it is not a ``validity oracle.'' Its results are sensitive to the choice of features, neighborhood definitions, and audit thresholds.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Absence of Closed-Loop Evaluation}: The current study evaluates LabelScope only through retrospective audits of public datasets. It does not evaluate the framework in a closed-loop HMS or with human participants. Determining how audit-triggered claim caps affect actual system behavior, user experience, and task performance requires rigorous user studies in future deployments.
    \item \textbf{Epistemological Limits of the Proxy Audit}: The PA only audits against \textit{observed} covariates ($C_B$). In unrestricted environments, physiological signals can be sensitive to unobserved factors (e.g., room temperature, undocumented postural shifts, caffeine intake). A ``Level 3'' claim indicates only that no evidence of proxy risk was found under the tested covariates; it cannot guarantee the absence of unmeasured entanglements. Future work should integrate causal inference techniques to bound the impact of unobserved variables.
    \item \textbf{Threshold Standardization and Sensitivity}: The default values of $\tau_{RA} = 5.0$ and $\tau_{PA} = 0.30$ were chosen conservatively. Table \ref{tab:pa_sensitivity} reports the PA cap outcome for WESAD across a range of $\tau_{PA}$ values. The Level 2 cap is robust across $\tau_{PA} \in [0.20, 0.60]$ under the group-mean rule, since the group mean ($\rho_{LC} = 0.65$) exceeds all thresholds in this range. At $\tau_{PA} = 0.70$, the group mean no longer exceeds the threshold; however, 6 of 15 subjects individually exceed it, warranting subject-level reporting in that regime.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented LabelScope, a conservative framework for auditing state-label validity in physiology-based HMS. By shifting the focus from predictive accuracy to claim supportability, LabelScope can support more cautious and reproducible reporting of physiological HMS claims. As HMS are deployed in increasingly consequential environments, systematic auditing of claim supportability will be an important step toward building human-centered systems with a well-characterized scope of inference.

\section*{Data and Code Availability}
The WESAD and SWELL-KW datasets are publicly available. We provide the preprocessing configuration, exact feature lists, and auditing scripts in the supplementary material to support peer review. LabelScope is designed to be reproducible under fixed preprocessing parameters and permutation seeds. The implementation uses NeuroKit2 (v0.2.1) and cvxEDA.
