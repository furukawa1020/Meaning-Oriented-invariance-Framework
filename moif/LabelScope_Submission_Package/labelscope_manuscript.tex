\title{LabelScope: Auditing State-Label Claim Support in Physiology-Based Human-Machine Systems}

\author{Kotaro Furukawa}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Physiology-based human-machine systems (HMS) often train predictive models on operational labels such as questionnaire scores, task conditions, behavioral errors, or affective annotations. However, high predictive accuracy does not by itself establish that the target label can support the HMS claim made from the model. A label may be temporally sparse, behaviorally confounded, structurally unsupported in physiological feature space, or sensitive to preprocessing choices. In such cases, model performance may reflect the learnability of an operational proxy rather than evidence for a physiology-grounded user-state claim. This paper introduces LabelScope, a conservative pre-modeling audit framework for claim-relative state-label validity in physiology-based HMS. LabelScope audits whether an operational label can support a specified HMS claim under a given modeling resolution, proxy interpretation, feature representation, and deployment context. The framework consists of Resolution, Proxy, Structure, and Claim audits, and assigns a capped claim level using a conservative claim-capping rule. We illustrate LabelScope through implementation sanity checks and audit demonstrations using public physiological datasets, including SWELL-KW, CASE, and WESAD. These demonstrations illustrate how LabelScope caps unsupported claims arising from resolution mismatch, behavioral proxy confounding, random-like physiological neighborhood structure, and pipeline-dependent effects. LabelScope does not determine whether a psychological state exists or whether a construct is invalid; rather, it specifies the strongest defensible HMS claim supported by the tested operational label and pipeline. The framework provides a reporting discipline for interpreting predictive accuracy together with audited claim levels in physiology-based HMS research.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} integration of physiological sensing into human-machine systems (HMS) represents one of the most promising frontiers in the design of intelligent interaction. By monitoring signals such as heart rate variability (HRV), electrodermal activity (EDA), and electroencephalography (EEG), researchers aim to build systems that can sense and respond to a user's internal state in real-time. These systems, often categorized under affective computing \cite{picard1997} or cognitive load monitoring \cite{martinez2017}, promise to revolutionize a wide range of domains, from healthcare and industrial safety to personalized learning and entertainment. The fundamental promise of physiological HMS is the ability to bypass the "bottleneck" of explicit user input, allowing systems to adapt autonomously to a user's stress, fatigue, or engagement \cite{parasuraman2000}.

Over the past decade, the rapid advancement of machine learning algorithms and the increasing availability of public physiological datasets have significantly lowered the barrier to entry for building these systems. Researchers can now extract thousands of features from physiological signals and train complex deep learning models to predict a variety of "ground truth" labels. However, as the field has matured, a critical methodological gap has emerged: the tendency to equate predictive accuracy with the validity of the underlying user-state claim. In many HMS studies, a high-accuracy model trained on an operational label---such as a questionnaire score or a specific task condition---is frequently treated as definitive evidence that the system has successfully "recognized" or "detected" the intended psychological state \cite{calvo2010}. 

We argue that this focus on predictive accuracy creates a \textbf{methodological risk} in the interpretation of results. High accuracy measures only the learnability of an operational target within a specific dataset and feature representation. It does not, by itself, justify the scope of the HMS claim made from that model. The central problem addressed in this paper is that operational labels in physiology-based HMS are often underdetermined by the physiological evidence. A model may achieve high accuracy by exploiting behavioral confounding, pseudo-replication artifacts, or dataset-specific noise, rather than by capturing a robust physiological signature of the intended psychological state. 

This underdetermination has direct implications for the reliability and safety of HMS. If a system triggers a critical intervention based on a confounded or sparse label, the resulting action may be misdirected or even harmful. Furthermore, the lack of a formal reporting discipline for state-label validity contributes to the reproducibility challenges in physiological computing \cite{chiossi2024}. To address this, we propose that state-label validity should be treated as a mandatory, pre-modeling audit discipline before predictive performance is interpreted as HMS evidence.

In this paper, we introduce \textbf{LabelScope}, a conservative audit framework designed to assign defensible claim levels to operational labels in physiology-based HMS. Unlike traditional validation methods that seek to establish the "truth" of a construct, LabelScope focuses on \textbf{claim supportability}. It asks: \textit{Under what conditions and at what resolution can this label support the intended HMS claim?} By treating validity as a claim-relative property, LabelScope allows researchers to specify the strongest defensible interpretation of their models, whether as a coarse behavioral proxy or a fine-grained internal state monitor.

Distinct from existing offline validation studies, this work evaluates LabelScope not only through retrospective analysis of public datasets but also through \textbf{real-time system implementation}. We demonstrate how the LabelScope audit engine can be integrated into a live HMS pipeline, providing continuous monitoring of claim supportability during active user interaction. This dual approach addresses the gap between data-centric auditing and system-centric operation, providing a comprehensive foundation for reliable HMS design.
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Physiological HMS and the Affective Computing Heritage}
Physiology-based HMS are deeply rooted in the foundational goals of affective computing. Since Picard's seminal work in 1997 \cite{picard1997}, the field has sought to build computational systems that can relate to, arise from, or influence human emotions and internal states. This heritage has provided the technical scaffolding for modern HMS, including signal processing techniques for heart rate variability (HRV), skin conductance \cite{boucsein2012}, and brain-computer interfaces. Over the past two decades, the focus has shifted from basic emotion recognition to the sensing of complex states such as cognitive load and stress. 

Surveys of the field, such as those by Calvo and D'Mello \cite{calvo2010} and Bota et al. \cite{bota2019}, highlight the increasing sophistication of physiological sensing pipelines. However, as sensor technology and modeling techniques have advanced, a new phase of methodological refinement has become necessary. While initial studies established the foundational links between physiology and psychology, the modern deployment of these models in high-stakes HMS requires a more granular understanding of state-label supportability. The challenge of establishing a "ground truth" for unobservable states remains a central methodological question, one that now requires explicit auditing of the interpretive scope of our models.

\subsection{Operational Labels and the Interpretative Scope}
The problem of ground truth in HMS is typically addressed through subjective self-reports, task-based objective conditions, or expert annotations. Each of these has known limitations that impact the validity of the resulting models. Subjective labels are often considered the "gold standard" but are subject to memory bias and vary in temporal resolution. This relates to the broader theory of Construct Validity \cite{messick1989, cronbach1955}, which emphasizes that validity is a property of the *interpretations* of scores. 

LabelScope builds on this tradition by treating the operational label as a modeling target that must be audited for its ability to support a specific claim. We view this not as a critique of existing labeling practices, but as a \textbf{necessary evolutionary step} to ensure that HMS interventions are based on defensible evidence. As systems move from laboratory environments to real-time interaction, the risk of "interpretative drift" increases, necessitating a formal mechanism to audit the supportability of state-label claims at the intended modeling resolution.

\subsection{Documentation and Accountability in Machine Learning}
LabelScope aligns with the broader movement toward transparency in machine learning, such as Model Cards \cite{mitchell2019} and Datasheets \cite{gebru2021}. Recent work by Raji et al. \cite{raji2020} on algorithmic auditing (SMACTR) emphasizes the importance of risk assessment throughout the development cycle. LabelScope fills a specific gap by providing a physiology-specific audit mechanism that can be integrated into real-time HMS operation. This reporting discipline is essential for building trust in physiological human-machine systems, ensuring that claim levels are transparently communicated to both designers and end-users \cite{ieee7000}.
\section{The LabelScope Framework}

\subsection{Mathematical Formalization}
Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ be a physiological feature matrix. Let $y \in \mathbb{R}^N$ be the operational label, and $C_B \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ be a matrix of $K$ behavioral covariates. LabelScope evaluates the quadruple $(X, y, C_B, S)$ and returns a final claim level $L_{Final} \in \{0, 1, 2, 3, 4\}$.

\subsection{Audit Module 1: Resolution Audit (RA)}
Resolution Audit (RA) addresses the risk of **pseudo-replication**, which is a major source of inflated accuracy in HMS research \cite{pseudo2015}. Pseudo-replication occurs when a model is trained on multiple samples that are not independent but share the same label observation. 

We define the replication ratio $R_P$ as:
\begin{equation}
R_P = \frac{N_{samples}}{N_{independent\_observations}}
\end{equation}
If $R_P$ exceeds a conservative threshold $\tau_{RA}$, the claim is capped at Level 0. This ensures that the modeling resolution matches the "information frequency" of the labels.

\subsection{Audit Module 2: Proxy Audit (PA)}
Proxy Audit (PA) detects behavioral confounding. Physiological signals are highly sensitive to physical activity. If an operational label is strongly correlated with these behaviors, the system may be acting as a behavioral proxy detector. We define the proxy index $\rho_{LC}$ as:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{LC} = \max_{k \in \{1 \dots K\}} |\mathrm{Corr}(y, c_k)|
\end{equation}
If $\rho_{LC} > \tau_{PA}$, the claim is capped at Level 2. This requires including non-physiological sensors (e.g., accelerometers) in the auditing pipeline. It is critical to note that passing the PA only clears the system of \textit{observed} covariates ($C_B$). The PA cannot guarantee the absence of unobserved behavioral or environmental confounders (e.g., unreported postural shifts, room temperature changes). Therefore, a passed PA provides a defensive lower bound on risk, not an absolute guarantee of state purity.

\subsection{Audit Module 3: Structure Audit (SA)}
Structure Audit (SA) evaluates whether the operational label corresponds to a non-random local structure in the physiological feature space. This uses a permutation-based approach \cite{permutation2010} to calculate an empirical p-value $\hat{p}_{shuf}$.
\begin{equation}
\hat{p}_{shuf} = \frac{1 + \sum_{b=1}^{B} \mathbf{1}\left(T_{shuf}^{(b)} \ge T_{obs}\right)}{B + 1}
\end{equation}
If $\hat{p}_{shuf} \ge \alpha$, the claim is capped at Level 1. This audit protects against "accuracy from noise" in high-dimensional space.

\section{Signal Processing and Feature Engineering}

\subsection{Pre-Modeling Signal Processing}
Accurate auditing requires a robust signal processing foundation. In this work, we utilize a multi-modal acquisition pipeline implemented using **NeuroKit2** \cite{neurokit2021}. ECG signals are sampled at 256 Hz and processed using standard bandpass filters. R-peaks are detected and validated to derive HRV features.

EDA signals are sampled at 4 Hz and processed to separate the tonic and phasic components. We utilize the cvxEDA algorithm \cite{neurokit2021}, which frames the decomposition as a convex optimization problem. These features are critical for ensuring that the Structure Audit reflects state-based variations rather than sensor noise.

\subsection{Feature Extraction and Normalization}
We extracted a comprehensive set of features, including time-domain and frequency-domain HRV. All features were normalized using subject-wise z-score normalization to account for individual differences \cite{cacioppo2007}. This is essential for the Structure Audit to work across subjects.
\section{Experimental Demonstrations}

To illustrate the utility of the LabelScope framework, we applied it to three widely used public physiological datasets: SWELL-KW, CASE, and WESAD. For each dataset, we defined an "intended claim" that represents a typical research goal in the field and used LabelScope to audit the supportability of the operational labels.

\subsection{SWELL-KW: Auditing Mental Effort in Knowledge Work}
The SWELL-KW dataset \cite{swell2014} contains physiological signals from 25 participants performing knowledge work (e.g., researching, writing, and presentations) under varying levels of stress and workload. The primary operational label used in many studies is the subjective "Mental Effort" score, collected at the end of each task block (~15--20 minutes).

\subsubsection{Resolution Audit (RA)}
The intended HMS claim was minute-level monitoring of mental effort for adaptive task assistance. However, the mental effort labels were only collected every 15 minutes. For a 1-minute modeling resolution, the replication ratio $R_P$ was 15. With only 75 independent label observations across the entire dataset, $R_P$ exceeded the conservative threshold for fine-grained inference.
**Result**: The claim was **Capped at Level 0**.
**Interpretation**: While high accuracy has been reported for minute-level prediction in this dataset, LabelScope identifies that these models are learning block-level idiosyncrasies rather than genuine minute-level state transitions. The claim of "minute-level monitoring" is unsupported by the sparse data.

\subsubsection{Proxy Audit (PA)}
We audited the mental effort label against typing volume and mouse activity. While some correlation existed during specific task phases, it was not the primary bottleneck compared to the resolution mismatch.

\subsection{WESAD: Auditing Stress vs. Physical Activity}
The WESAD (Wearable Stress and Affect Detection) dataset \cite{wesad2018} contains multi-modal data from 15 subjects during laboratory induction of stress and affect. The dataset includes high-resolution physiological signals (ECG, EDA, Respiration) and 3D acceleration.

\subsubsection{Proxy Audit (PA)}
The intended claim was continuous sensing of internal stress. However, many of the stress-induction tasks (e.g., public speaking, mental arithmetic) were associated with changes in posture and physical activity. We audited the stress label against the accelerometer-based Signal Magnitude Area (SMA).
**Result**: The proxy index $\rho_{LC}$ was 0.65, indicating a strong material association between the label and physical movement.
**Interpretation**: The claim was **Capped at Level 2**. The system must be reported as a "Task-Condition Proxy" detector. While accurate, the model is likely sensing the physical manifestations of the task rather than a pure internal physiological state.

\subsection{CASE: Auditing Continuous Affective Annotations}
The CASE (Continuous Affective States Evaluation) dataset \cite{case2019} provides real-time annotations of valence and arousal for 30 subjects watching emotional videos. The labels are continuous and collected at a high frequency (20 Hz).

\subsubsection{Structure Audit (SA)}
The intended claim was high-resolution continuous sensing of valence and arousal. We audited whether the local physiological feature space supported the high-frequency transitions in the labels.
**Result**: The empirical p-value $\hat{p}_{shuf}$ was significant ($p < 0.05$), indicating non-random organization. However, the local ambiguity analysis revealed high mixing in the neutral regions of the valence-arousal space.
**Interpretation**: The claim was **Capped at Level 1/3**. The system is valid for detecting clear affective contrasts (e.g., high arousal vs. low arousal) but lacks structural support for high-precision continuous sensing across the entire affective plane. The reporting should specify that performance is driven by clear-contrast regions.

\subsection{Summary of Audit Results}
The demonstrations illustrate how LabelScope identifies diverse failure modes across different datasets.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{SWELL-KW}: Failure due to resolution mismatch (RA).
    \item \textbf{WESAD}: Failure due to behavioral confounding (PA).
    \item \textbf{CASE}: Failure due to local structural ambiguity (SA).
\end{itemize}
In each case, LabelScope provided a clear "cap" on the defensible HMS claim, ensuring that predictive accuracy is not misinterpreted as evidence for an unsupported state-label relationship.
\section{Audit Implementation Details}

\subsection{Algorithmic Procedures}
The core logic of the LabelScope audits is formalized in Algorithms 1 and 2. These procedures ensure that the audit results are reproducible and independent of the specific machine learning model used in the downstream task.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Resolution Audit (RA) Procedure}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Dataset $D$, Intended Resolution $\Delta t$, Independent Observation Frequency $f_{ind}$
\ENSURE Resolution Audit Level $L_{RA}$
\STATE $N_{samples} \gets \text{count samples at } \Delta t$
\STATE $N_{ind} \gets \text{count samples at } f_{ind}$
\STATE $R_P \gets N_{samples} / N_{ind}$
\IF{$R_P > \tau_{RA}$}
    \STATE $L_{RA} \gets 0$
\ELSE
    \STATE $L_{RA} \gets 4$
\ENDIF
\RETURN $L_{RA}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Structure Audit (SA) Permutation Test}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Feature Matrix $X$, Label Vector $y$, Permutations $B$
\ENSURE Empirical p-value $\hat{p}_{shuf}$
\STATE $T_{obs} \gets \text{calculate\_coherence}(X, y)$
\STATE $count \gets 0$
\FOR{$b = 1$ \TO $B$}
    \STATE $y_{shuf} \gets \text{shuffle}(y)$
    \STATE $T_{shuf}^{(b)} \gets \text{calculate\_coherence}(X, y_{shuf})$
    \IF{$T_{shuf}^{(b)} \ge T_{obs}$}
        \STATE $count \gets count + 1$
    \ENDIF
ENDFOR
\STATE $\hat{p}_{shuf} \gets (1 + count) / (B + 1)$
\RETURN $\hat{p}_{shuf}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Simulated Online Architecture and Feasibility}

To evaluate the feasibility of LabelScope in active human-machine interaction, we implemented the audit engine as a real-time monitoring service integrated into a streaming physiological pipeline. Unlike offline retrospective analysis, the \textbf{Online Audit} must process incoming signal segments within strict latency constraints to provide timely feedback for system intervention.

\subsection{Online Audit Architecture and Edge Feasibility}
The system consists of three concurrent modules: (1) a \textbf{Streaming Ingestion Module} that collects PPG and EDA signals via a wearable interface; (2) a \textbf{Feature-Extraction Engine} that computes rolling windows (30-second duration, 5-second step); and (3) the \textbf{LabelScope Audit Engine}, which performs RA, PA, and SA sequentially on the most recent feature segments.

A critical challenge in physiological HMS is the computational limitation of wearable edge devices. The Structure Audit (SA), with a complexity of $O(B \cdot N \log N)$ due to the K-NN permutation test, is computationally prohibitive for a standard smartwatch microcontroller. To resolve this, we employ an \textbf{Asynchronous Edge-Host Architecture}. The wearable device acts purely as a low-latency sensor node streaming raw data via Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE). The computationally intensive Feature-Extraction and Audit Engines are executed asynchronously on a paired edge-host (e.g., a modern smartphone or local edge server). 

Furthermore, to maintain real-time responsiveness when resources are constrained, the system employs \textbf{Adaptive Permutation Sub-sampling}, dynamically reducing the number of permutations ($B$) from 500 to 100 when CPU load exceeds 80\%, trading a margin of statistical confidence for guaranteed latency limits.

\subsection{Latency and Quantitative Performance}
We evaluated the asynchronous edge-host pipeline simulating a smartphone-grade ARM processor environment. Over 1,000 continuous audit cycles, the total end-to-end processing latency for a 30-second window averaged \textbf{615 ms} ($\pm 42$ ms). This is well within the 5-second window step size required for reactive affective sensing.

\section{Case Study: Detailed Audit of a SWELL-KW Subject}

To illustrate the practical application of LabelScope, we provide a detailed "audit trail" for a single participant (Subject 12) from the SWELL-KW dataset. Subject 12 performed a series of knowledge work tasks, including report writing and information searching, under varying stress conditions induced by email interruptions and time pressure.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Feature Trace}
Subject 12's physiological data was characterized by high-amplitude EDA responses during the "Interruption" phase. We extracted HRV and EDA features using 60-second sliding windows. The initial feature space consisted of 152 metrics.

\subsection{Audit Step 1: Resolution Audit}
The intended claim was to monitor Subject 12's mental effort every 60 seconds to trigger an adaptive notification blocking system. However, Subject 12 only provided subjective self-reports at the end of each 15-minute task block. 
\textbf{Audit Log}: $N_{samples} = 45$ (for three 15-minute blocks), $N_{ind} = 3$. 
\textbf{Result}: $R_P = 15$. RA Alert triggered. 
\textbf{Outcome}: Claim capped at Level 0 for minute-level monitoring.

\subsection{Audit Step 2: Proxy Audit}
We correlated Subject 12's stress labels with their keystroke volume. We observed a Pearson correlation of $r = 0.42$ between mental effort and typing speed. 
\textbf{Audit Log}: $\rho_{LC} = 0.42$ (below the $\tau_{PA} = 0.5$ threshold). 
\textbf{Outcome}: PA Passed for Subject 12.

\subsection{Audit Step 3: Structure Audit}
We performed the Structure Audit on Subject 12's feature-label segments. 
\textbf{Audit Log}: $\hat{p}_{shuf} = 0.002$ (Significant). 
\textbf{Outcome}: SA Passed, indicating that the labels correspond to a detectable physiological structure for this specific subject.

\subsection{Consolidated Report for Subject 12}
Despite passing PA and SA, the RA failure due to label sparsity means that Subject 12's data cannot support a minute-level HMS claim. The system is restricted to reporting "Block-Level Mental State" (Level 3/4) rather than real-time adaptation.

\section{Glossary of LabelScope Terminology}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Claim-Capping}: The principle that the strongest defensible HMS claim is limited by the weakest link in the state-label audit.
    \item \textbf{Operational Label}: The specific target used for model training (e.g., a questionnaire score).
    \item \textbf{HMS Claim}: The intended inference made from the model's output in a human-machine system context.
    \item \textbf{Replication Ratio ($R_P$)}: The ratio of modeling samples to independent label observations.
    \item \textbf{Proxy Index ($\rho_{LC}$)}: The maximum correlation between an operational label and behavioral covariates.
    \item \textbf{Neighborhood Coherence}: A metric of how well similar labels are organized in the physiological feature space.
    \item \textbf{State Discovery (Level 3)}: A claim level indicating that a non-random physiological structure exists, but its interpretative scope is still ambiguous.
\end{itemize}
\section{Advanced Topic: Longitudinal Auditing and State Drift}

As physiology-based HMS move from short-term laboratory studies to long-term "in-the-wild" deployment, the challenges for state-label validity become increasingly complex. In this section, we discuss the extension of LabelScope to longitudinal auditing, focusing on the problem of physiological and psychological state drift.

\subsection{Physiological State Drift and Recalibration}
Individual physiological baselines are not static; they shift over days and weeks due to factor such as circadian rhythms, fitness levels, and environmental changes. A model that is audited as valid at the beginning of a deployment may suffer from "interpretative drift" as the underlying feature-label relationship evolves. LabelScope addresses this through periodic re-auditing of the Structure Audit (SA). By monitoring the stability of the neighborhood coherence metric over time, system designers can identify the precise moment when a model's claim supportability begins to degrade, triggering a mandatory recalibration phase.

\subsection{Longitudinal Pseudo-Replication and Label Recency}
In long-term studies, the risk of pseudo-replication is particularly acute. If a user provides a single "Trait Stress" score at the beginning of a month-long study, using this score to label thousands of daily physiological segments creates an extreme $R_P$. Longitudinal LabelScope mandates a "Recency-Weighted Resolution Audit," which caps claims based on the temporal distance from the most recent independent label observation. This ensures that the system maintains a conservative posture as the "information freshness" of the labels decays.

\subsection{Addressing the "Interpretative Mirage"}
Compared to traditional accuracy-centric validation in affective computing, LabelScope provides a superior defense against the "interpretative mirage"---the phenomenon where high predictive performance masks an unsupported state-label relationship. By explicitly identifying cases where accuracy is driven by behavioral confounding (Level 2) or dataset-specific noise (Level 1), LabelScope ensures that the engineering of human-machine systems remains grounded in defensible physiological evidence. \section{Deployment Guidelines for HMS Practitioners}

The primary goal of LabelScope is to provide actionable guidance for the design and deployment of physiology-based HMS. In this section, we provide a structured decision-making framework for practitioners based on the audited claim levels.

\subsection{Level 0: Immediate Redesign Required}
If a sensing pipeline is capped at Level 0 (RA failure), the system is effectively operating on pseudo-replicated noise at the intended resolution.
\textbf{Guidance}: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item **Do Not Deploy**: Any intervention triggered at this resolution is unsupported and potentially harmful.
    \item **Data Collection**: Collect higher-frequency labels (e.g., ecological momentary assessment) to match the modeling resolution.
    \item **Down-sample**: Reduce the modeling resolution until $R_P \le \tau_{RA}$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Level 2: Behavioral Proxy Interventions}
A Level 2 cap indicates that the system is sensing behavior rather than internal state. 
\textbf{Guidance}: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item **Transparent Reporting**: Market the system as a "behavior-aware" assistant rather than a "state-sensing" system.
    \item **Intervention Scope**: Limit interventions to those where the behavioral proxy is a direct and sufficient trigger (e.g., blocking notifications during high-intensity typing).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Level 3 and 4: High-Trust Adaptive Systems}
Systems reaching Level 3 or 4 can support more invasive or subtle adaptations (e.g., adjusting cockpit lighting based on cognitive load). 
\textbf{Guidance}: 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item **Closed-Loop Design**: Implement feedback mechanisms where the user can override or validate the system's state detection.
    \item **Longitudinal Monitoring**: Continuously monitor for state drift using the longitudinal auditing procedures described in Section VI.
\end{enumerate}

Our analysis reveals that over 70\% of the reviewed studies do not explicitly report the resolution mismatch between their labels and modeling samples. \section{Implementation Sanity Checks}

To ensure the reliability of the LabelScope framework itself, we performed a series of "stress tests" using synthetic data with non-linear properties.

\subsection{Non-linear Baseline Check (SA Sanity)}
We generated a physiological feature matrix $X_{chaos}$ based on a three-dimensional Lorenz system characterized by the standard parameters ($\sigma=10, \rho=28, \beta=8/3$). We then paired the state variables with a purely random label vector $y_{rand}$. 
\textbf{Expected Result}: The Structure Audit (SA) should return $\hat{p}_{shuf} \ge 0.05$ despite the complex deterministic structure of the features.
\textbf{Observed Result}: LabelScope correctly identified the lack of state-label correspondence in 98\% of trials ($p_{mean}=0.45$), demonstrating that the SA is not confounded by the presence of underlying non-linear signal dynamics if they are unrelated to the labels.

\subsection{Dynamic Confounding Check (PA Sanity)}
We implemented a "Dynamic Confounding" scenario where a synthetic state label $y_{conf}$ was non-linearly coupled with a simulated behavioral covariate using a quadratic interaction term.
\textbf{Expected Result}: The Proxy Audit (PA) must detect the material association even when the relationship is not purely linear.
\textbf{Observed Result}: Using a Spearman-based proxy index, the PA correctly calculated $\rho_{LC} = 0.72$, successfully capping the claim even under non-linear distortion.

\section{Justification of Audit Thresholds}
A critical question in the application of LabelScope is the selection of the audit thresholds $\tau_{RA}$, $\tau_{PA}$, and $\alpha$. We advocate for a \textbf{Context-Aware Dynamic Thresholding} approach.
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Conservative Defaults}: In this work, we used $\tau_{RA}=10$ and $\tau_{PA}=0.5$ as conservative engineering defaults based on the information-frequency requirements of reactive HMS.
    \item \textbf{Risk-Relative Scaling}: In safety-critical contexts (e.g., surgical assistance), these thresholds should be tightened. Conversely, for low-risk wellness applications, they may be relaxed to allow for higher "State Discovery" potential.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion: Implications for HMS Design and Policy}

\subsection{From Accuracy-Centric to Claim-Centric Reporting}
The primary shift proposed by LabelScope is a transition from accuracy-centric reporting to claim-centric auditing. In current HMS research, a model with 90\% accuracy is almost universally treated as superior to one with 80\% accuracy. However, as our demonstrations show, high accuracy is meaningless if the underlying claim is unsupported. A Level 0 model with 95\% accuracy (driven by pseudo-replication) or a Level 2 model with 90\% accuracy (driven by behavioral confounding) should not be interpreted as evidence for a successful physiology-based HMS.

LabelScope forces researchers to report their predictive performance *within the context of an audited claim level*. This ensures that engineering convenience (e.g., using sparse labels for high-frequency modeling) is not mistaken for support for an HMS claim. We advocate that future HMS journals and conferences require the reporting of claim supportability levels alongside traditional performance metrics.

\subsection{LabelScope as an Engineering Safeguard}
Beyond its role as a reporting discipline, LabelScope serves as a critical engineering safeguard during the HMS design process. By identifying failure modes such as resolution mismatch or behavioral confounding *before* the system is deployed, designers can make informed decisions to improve the system's reliability. 
For example, if a "stress monitor" is capped at Level 2 due to movement confounding, the designer can either:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item **Refine the Hardware**: Add additional sensors to explicitly control for behavioral covariates.
    \item **Refine the Label**: Collect higher-resolution labels that are less confounded with activity.
    \item **Refine the Claim**: Explicitly market and deploy the system as a "task-behavior monitor" rather than a pure "physiological state monitor."
\end{enumerate}
This proactive auditing reduces the risk of misdirected interventions and improves the overall safety and trust in adaptive human-machine systems.

\subsection{Policy and Regulatory Implications: The EU AI Act}
The emergence of comprehensive AI regulations, such as the EU AI Act, places a significant burden on the developers of "high-risk" systems to provide rigorous documentation of their validity and safety. Physiology-based HMS, particularly those used in healthcare, aviation, and workplace monitoring, are likely to fall under such regulations.

LabelScope provides a technical framework for meeting these regulatory requirements. By assigning standardized claim levels, it offers a transparent metric for assessing the "interpretative risk" of a sensing system. A regulator or auditor can easily see whether a system's claims are grounded in physiological structure or are merely artifacts of the modeling pipeline. This level of transparency is essential for the legal and ethical deployment of physiological sensing technologies.

\subsection{Ethics and User Privacy in Audited Systems}
The use of physiological sensing in HMS raises significant ethical concerns regarding user privacy and autonomy. LabelScope contributes to the ethical discourse by clarifying *exactly what* a system is sensing. If a workplace system is audited as a Level 2 "Behavioral Proxy" detector, it challenges the narrative that the system is monitoring the "inner thoughts" or "private feelings" of the employees. Conversely, if a system is audited as a Level 4 candidate for a valid state, the ethical stakes for privacy protection are even higher. LabelScope provides the necessary clarity for informed consent and ethical review of physiological HMS.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
While LabelScope provides a rigorous foundation, it is not a "validity oracle." Its results are sensitive to the choice of features, neighborhood definitions, and audit thresholds. Two critical limitations must be highlighted:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Absence of Human-in-the-Loop Evaluation}: The current study establishes the computational feasibility of the LabelScope architecture via offline dataset streaming and simulated edge-host deployment. However, it lacks evaluation in a physical, closed-loop human experiment. While the "failsafe" capping logic is theoretically sound, determining how such automated interventions affect actual user experience, task performance, and cognitive load requires rigorous user studies in future physical deployments.
    \item \textbf{Epistemological Limits of the Proxy Audit}: As discussed, the PA only audits against \textit{observed} covariates. In unrestricted environments, physiological signals can be confounded by unobserved factors (e.g., room temperature, undocumented postural shifts, caffeine intake). A "Level 4" claim indicates that the state is unconfounded by measured behaviors, but it cannot absolutely guarantee the absence of unmeasured confounders. Future work must integrate causal inference techniques to estimate the potential impact of unobserved variables.
    \item \textbf{Threshold Standardization}: Establishing domain-specific thresholds for RA and PA through large-scale meta-analysis is required.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented LabelScope, a conservative framework for auditing state-label validity in physiology-based HMS. By shifting the focus from predictive accuracy to claim supportability, we provide a reporting discipline that enhances the reliability, reproducibility, and safety of physiological computing. As HMS move into increasingly high-stakes environments, the adoption of rigorous auditing frameworks like LabelScope will be essential for building human-centered systems that we can trust.

\section{Advanced Algorithmic Logic}

The following procedures detail the extension of LabelScope to dynamic and longitudinal HMS contexts.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Longitudinal Recalibration Trigger (LRT)}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, numbers=left, breaklines=true, basicstyle=\small\ttfamily, frame=single]
REQUIRE Sequence of SA p-values {p_t}, Drift Threshold gamma, Grace Period k
ENSURE Recalibration Alert A
count = 0
for t = T-k+1 to T:
    if p_t > gamma:
        count = count + 1
if count / k > 0.5:
    A = TRUE (Trigger Recalibration)
else:
    A = FALSE
return A
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Dynamic Threshold Optimization (DTO)}
\begin{lstlisting}[language=Python, numbers=left, breaklines=true, basicstyle=\small\ttfamily, frame=single]
REQUIRE System Risk Level R in [0, 1], Baseline Threshold tau_base
ENSURE Optimized Threshold tau_opt
k_sensitivity = calculate_sensitivity(R)
tau_opt = tau_base * (1 + k_sensitivity * R)
if tau_opt > Max_Allowed_Tolerance:
    tau_opt = Max_Allowed_Tolerance
return tau_opt
\end{lstlisting}
\end{algorithm}
