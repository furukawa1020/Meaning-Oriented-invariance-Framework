\title{LabelScope: A Claim-Capping Framework for Auditing Operational Labels in Physiology-Based Human-Machine Systems}

\author{Kotaro Furukawa}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Physiology-based human-machine systems (HMS) often report high predictive performance on operational labels, but such labels may not support the state claims used to drive adaptive system behavior. Rather than evaluating model performance alone, LabelScope audits whether an operational label can support the intended HMS claim. The framework assigns an interpretive claim level $L_{Final} \in \{0,1,2,3\}$ using Resolution (RA), Proxy (PA), and Structure (SA) audit modules. The framework is demonstrated through case studies on the WESAD and SWELL-KW datasets. In WESAD, a momentary 60-s internal-state claim was capped at Level 0 by RA, while, for a protocol-linked stress-proxy reporting claim, a computed Level 2 cap was assigned due to behavioral entanglement ($\rho_{LC} = 0.68$ at protocol level). In SWELL-KW, a redesigned 15-min block-level claim resolved the RA failure but remained subject to a PA-imposed Level 2 upper cap, showing that coarser claim redesign does not automatically rescue physiological validity from behavioral entanglement.
By flagging failure modes such as behavioral-proxy risk and pseudo-replication under specified audit assumptions, LabelScope provides an audit basis for cautious HMS design, supporting the interpretation of predictive accuracy within defensible claim boundaries.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\IEEEPARstart{P}{hysiological} sensing is increasingly integrated into human-machine systems (HMS) to infer operator state without requiring explicit input. By monitoring signals such as heart rate variability (HRV), electrodermal activity (EDA), and electroencephalography (EEG), researchers aim to build systems that can adapt to a user's stress, fatigue, or cognitive load \cite{picard1997,martinez2017,cheng2020}. These systems, often positioned under affective computing or cognitive load monitoring, have been applied across domains including healthcare, industrial safety, and assistive technology \cite{parasuraman2000}.

Over the past decade, the rapid advancement of machine learning algorithms and the increasing availability of public physiological datasets have significantly lowered the barrier to entry for building these systems. Researchers can now extract thousands of features from physiological signals and train complex deep learning models to predict a variety of "ground truth" labels. However, as the field has matured, a critical methodological gap has emerged: the tendency to equate predictive accuracy with the validity of the underlying user-state claim. In many HMS studies, a high-accuracy model trained on an operational label---such as a questionnaire score or a specific task condition---is frequently treated as definitive evidence that the system has successfully "recognized" or "detected" the intended psychological state \cite{Calvo2010}. 

We argue that this focus on predictive accuracy creates a \textbf{methodological risk} in the interpretation of results. High accuracy measures only the learnability of an operational target within a specific dataset and feature representation. It does not, by itself, justify the scope of the HMS claim made from that model. The central problem addressed in this paper is that operational labels in physiology-based HMS are often underdetermined by the physiological evidence. A model may achieve high accuracy by exploiting behavioral-proxy risk, pseudo-replication artifacts, or dataset-specific noise, rather than by capturing a stable physiological signature of the intended psychological state. 

This underdetermination has direct consequences for HMS reliability. In a closed-loop HMS, the system's action policy is coupled to the inferred user state. If a system triggers an intervention based on an entangled label---for example, an adaptive learning environment incorrectly lowering task difficulty because it conflated a student's physical posture shift (a Level 2 behavioral proxy) for cognitive overload (a Level 3 internal state)---the resulting action disrupts the user's workflow and may erode trust in system autonomy \cite{parasuraman2000}. To prevent such failures, state-label validity must be treated as a systematic pre-interpretation audit discipline before predictive performance is allowed to govern system behavior.

This paper introduces \textbf{LabelScope}, a systematic claim-relative audit framework that evaluates whether a physiological operational label can support an intended HMS state claim. The primary contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Claim-Capping Formulation}: A formal framework that defines the upper bound of defensible HMS claims based on label supportability rather than predictive accuracy.
    \item \textbf{Audit Protocol}: The development of RA, PA, and SA modules to detect resolution mismatch, behavioral-proxy risk, and unsupported physiological structure.
    \item \textbf{Retrospective Case-Study Evaluation}: Demonstration on WESAD for claim capping under behavioral-proxy risk and on SWELL-KW for resolution-mismatch auditing, showing that high model performance can coexist with restrictive claim caps.
\end{enumerate}
These case studies illustrate how LabelScope can cap overbroad state claims under specified audit assumptions, providing an audit basis for constraining the system's action policy.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Physiological HMS and the Affective Computing Heritage}
Physiology-based HMS are deeply rooted in the foundational goals of affective computing. Since Picard's seminal work in 1997 \cite{picard1997}, the field has sought to build computational systems that can relate to, arise from, or influence human emotions and internal states. This heritage has provided the technical scaffolding for modern HMS, including signal processing techniques for heart rate variability (HRV), skin conductance \cite{boucsein2012}, and brain-computer interfaces. Over the past two decades, the focus has shifted from basic emotion recognition to the sensing of complex states such as cognitive load and stress \cite{zhang2024}. 

Surveys of the field, such as those by Calvo and D'Mello \cite{Calvo2010} and Bota et al. \cite{Bota2019}, highlight the increasing sophistication of physiological sensing pipelines. However, as sensor technology and modeling techniques have advanced, a new phase of methodological refinement has become necessary. While initial studies established the foundational links between physiology and psychology, the deployment of these models in adaptive HMS requires a more granular understanding of state-label supportability. The challenge of establishing a ``ground truth'' for unobservable states remains a central methodological question, one that now requires explicit auditing of the interpretive scope of our models.

\subsection{Adaptive Automation and Human-Machine Teaming}
The ultimate goal of physiological HMS is to enable adaptive automation, where the system dynamically adjusts its intervention (e.g., task offloading, alert suppression) based on the operator's cognitive or affective state \cite{parasuraman2000,kaber2018}. A critical challenge in human-machine teaming is trust calibration \cite{lee2004,lohani2019}. When an adaptive system intervenes based on a spurious state inference, it may degrade operator trust and situational awareness. LabelScope addresses this risk through a pre-intervention audit, providing a basis for constraining the system's action policy according to the audited claim level, which may help reduce the risk of trust degradation by preventing over-interpretation of unverified operational proxies.

\subsection{Construct Validity and the Ground Truth Problem}
The problem of ground truth in affective and physiological computing is notoriously difficult \cite{Calvo2010}. Systems typically rely on subjective self-reports or task-based objective conditions. This challenge relates to the broader theory of Construct Validity \cite{messick1989}, which emphasizes that validity is a property of the \textit{interpretations} of scores, not the scores themselves. LabelScope does not replace construct validation; it provides computable audit checks for whether an operational label can support a specified HMS claim under a given modeling setup. We treat the operational label as a weak or proxy label \cite{zhou2018} that should be audited for its supportability at a specific resolution before state claims are made.

Recent literature has highlighted severe methodological flaws in physiological ML, particularly data leakage through pseudo-replication (where overlapping windows from the same label observation inflate accuracy) \cite{pseudo2015} and behavioral-proxy risk (where physiological sensors act as high-variance motion detectors) \cite{chiossi2024}. 

While existing reporting frameworks such as Model Cards \cite{mitchell2019} and Datasheets \cite{gebru2021} document dataset and model properties, they do not prescribe an algorithmic bound on the physiological claim that can be supported by a given label. Table \ref{tab:framework_comparison} summarizes the distinction between LabelScope and these established frameworks. LabelScope addresses this gap by integrating known failure modes into a rule-based audit pipeline that outputs a \textbf{claim cap}---a computable ceiling on the HMS claim supported by the tested label, features, and covariates.

Let $X \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ be a physiological feature matrix derived exclusively from physiological modalities. Let $y$ be an operational label. Let $C_B \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times K}$ be behavioral covariates. LabelScope evaluates the tuple $(X, y, C_B, S)$ and returns $L_{Final} = \min(L_{RA}, L_{PA}, L_{SA})$, where $S$ denotes the intended HMS claim specification, including the target claim, modeling resolution, independent label unit, and permitted action scope.

\subsection{Audit Module 1: Resolution Audit (RA)}
RA addresses the risk of \textbf{pseudo-replication} \cite{pseudo2015}. We define the replication ratio $R_P = N_{samples}/N_{independent\_obs}$. If $R_P > \tau_{RA}$, the claim is capped at Level 0 (Unsupported Resolution).

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Resolution Audit (RA)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $N_{samples}, N_{ind\_obs}, \tau_{RA}$
\STATE $R_P \gets N_{samples} / N_{ind\_obs}$
\STATE $L_{RA} \gets (R_P > \tau_{RA}) ? 0 : 3$
\RETURN $L_{RA}, R_P$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Audit Module 2: Proxy Audit (PA)}
Proxy Audit (PA) detects \textbf{behavioral-proxy risk}. Physiological signals are highly sensitive to physical activity; if an operational label is strongly correlated with behavioral covariates (e.g., posture shifts or task actions), the system may be acting as a behavioral proxy detector rather than an internal-state monitor. We define the subject-wise proxy index $\rho_{LC,s}$ for subject $s$ as:
\begin{equation}
\rho_{LC,s} = \max_{k \in \{1 \dots K\}} |\mathrm{Spearman}(y_s, c_{s,k})|
\end{equation}
where $y_s$ is the label vector and $\{c_{s,k}\}$ are observed behavioral covariates for subject $s$. The group-level index $\rho_{LC}$ is calculated as the mean of subject-wise indices. If $\rho_{LC}$ exceeds the threshold $\tau_{PA}$, the claim is capped at Level 2. Critically, passing the PA only clears the system of \textit{observed} covariates; it cannot guarantee the absence of unobserved behavioral or environmental entanglements.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Proxy Audit (PA)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $\{y_s\}, \{c_{s,k}\}, \tau_{PA}$
\FOR{each subject $s$}
    \STATE $\rho_{LC,s} \gets \max_k |\text{Spearman}(y_s, c_{s,k})|$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\rho_{LC} \gets \frac{1}{S}\sum_s \rho_{LC,s}$
\STATE $L_{PA} \gets 2$ if $\rho_{LC} > \tau_{PA}$ else $3$
\RETURN $L_{PA}, \rho_{LC}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Audit Module 3: Structure Audit (SA)}
Structure Audit (SA) evaluates neighborhood coherence $T_{obs}$ in the physiological feature space $X$, which must be disjoint from $C_B$. We define $T_{obs}$ as the $k$-Nearest Neighbors (k-NN) label coherence:
\begin{equation}
T_{obs} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\left(y_i = \text{mode}(\{y_j\}_{j \in \mathcal{N}_k(x_i)})\right)
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{N}_k(x_i)$ is the set of $k$ nearest neighbors of $x_i$ using Euclidean distance after subject-wise z-score normalization. To account for temporal autocorrelation, the permutation test uses \textbf{block-shuffling}. The label time-series is divided into contiguous task blocks of length $L$, which are permuted within each subject before calculating the surrogate coherence $T_{shuf}^{(b)}$. The empirical p-value is calculated over $B$ permutations. If $\hat{p}_{shuf} \ge \alpha$, the claim is capped at Level 1.

\begin{algorithm}
\caption{Structure Audit (SA)}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE $X, y, B, \alpha$
\STATE $T_{obs} \gets \text{calc\_coherence}(X, y)$
\FOR{$b = 1$ \TO $B$}
    \STATE $y_{shuf} \gets \text{block\_shuffle}(y)$
    \STATE $T_{shuf}^{(b)} \gets \text{calc\_coherence}(X, y_{shuf})$
\ENDFOR
\STATE $\hat{p}_{shuf} \gets (1 + \sum [T_{shuf} \ge T_{obs}]) / (B+1)$
\STATE $L_{SA} \gets (\hat{p}_{shuf} \ge \alpha) ? 1 : 3$
\RETURN $L_{SA}, \hat{p}_{shuf}$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Claim Level Assignment and Mapping Justification}
The interpretive claim level is determined by the most restrictive constraint:
\begin{equation}
    L_{Final} = \min(L_{RA},\; L_{PA},\; L_{SA})
\end{equation}
Table \ref{tab:claim_levels} specifies the designation, cap condition, permitted HMS claim, and prohibited interpretation for each level. The mapping of audit failures to specific levels is justified by the following propositions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{L0 (Resolution Mismatch)}: If the replication ratio $R_P$ is too high, the target resolution itself is empirically unsupported, prohibiting any claim at that resolution.
    \item \textbf{L1 (Structure Mismatch)}: If the label lacks detectable organization in the physiological feature space (SA fail), the audit provides no basis for generalizing the label as a physiological state claim, limiting the claim to dataset- or context-specific monitoring.
    \item \textbf{L2 (Proxy Mismatch)}: If the label is behaviorally entangled (PA fail), the model cannot be interpreted as an internal-state monitor and should be reported as a behavior/task-level proxy.
    \item \textbf{L3 (Passed)}: No evidence was found to cap the claim under the tested configuration.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Retrospective Case-Study Evaluation}

\subsection{Experimental Protocol and Dataset Parameters}

\subsubsection{WESAD}
WESAD \cite{wesad2018} is a multimodal wearable dataset collected from 15 subjects (S2--S17, $N=15$). For this audit, we use chest-worn ECG and EDA signals for the Structure Audit and wrist-worn accelerometer-derived SMA as the behavioral covariate for the Proxy Audit. The binary label is defined as Stress ($y=1$) vs.~Non-Stress ($y=0$), where Non-Stress comprises both Baseline and Amusement conditions. Signal processing used a 60\,s sliding window with 50\% overlap ($\Delta t = 60\,\text{s}$, $n_{win} = 1110$ total windows). Feature extraction yielded $D=14$ physiology-only features. For SA, subject-wise z-scoring was used as an audit-specific normalization to evaluate localized feature-space organization independently of subject-level baseline offsets. This is distinct from the classifier baseline preprocessing, where normalization parameters were estimated using training subjects only and applied to the held-out subject in the LOSO scheme to prevent data leakage and simulate real-world deployment. The SMA covariate is computed with gravity removed. Audit thresholds: $\tau_{RA}=5.0$, $\tau_{PA}=0.30$, $\alpha=0.05$, $k=50$, $B=1000$.

\subsubsection{SWELL-KW}
SWELL-KW \cite{swell2014} contains physiological recordings from 25 participants under Neutral, Time Pressure, and Interruption conditions. For the redesigned 15-minute block claim, windowing is coarsened to one feature vector per block per subject ($R_P = 1$). For SWELL-KW PA, $C_B$ was defined as the aggregate computer interaction intensity (total mouse clicks and keystrokes) per 15-min block. The resulting protocol-level proxy index was $\rho_{LC}=0.42$, exceeding $\tau_{PA}=0.30$. 

\subsection{WESAD Case Study: Resolution and Proxy Risk}

\subsubsection{Resolution Audit (RA) Results}
For WESAD, the RA was evaluated under a claim of momentary 60-s internal-state detection. Because the operational label is derived from protocol blocks (Stress, Baseline, Amusement), we define $N_{ind\_obs}$ as the number of independent label segments across all subjects ($N_{ind\_obs} = 15 \text{ subjects} \times 3 \text{ blocks} = 45$). The replication ratio $R_P = 1110/45 \approx 24.7$ substantially exceeds the threshold $\tau_{RA} = 5.0$. Therefore, the momentary claim is capped at \textbf{Level 0} by RA. 

We subsequently evaluate a \textbf{protocol-linked stress-proxy claim}. For this claim, the unit of analysis is the 45 protocol-level blocks ($R_P = 1.0$). We re-ran the PA and SA modules on these aggregated protocol-level units. As shown in Table \ref{tab:wesad_claim_outcomes}, the protocol-level PA resulted in $\rho_{LC} = 0.68$, confirming that the Level 2 cap is a computed property of the protocol-linked claim rather than a post-hoc interpretation.

\subsubsection{Structure Audit (SA) Results}
The structure audit evaluated k-NN coherence in chest ECG/EDA feature space ($D=14$, $k=50$, $B=1000$, $L=60$\,s blocks, seed=42). To ensure that the coherence was not driven by temporal autocorrelation of adjacent windows, the k-NN candidate set for each query $x_i$ was restricted to windows outside the immediate $\pm 60$\,s temporal neighborhood. Furthermore, a cross-subject SA was performed by restricting the k-NN candidate set to windows from other subjects only; the resulting coherence remained statistically significant ($T_{obs}=0.64$, $p < 0.01$), suggesting that the observed organization is not solely driven by subject-specific temporal drift, although protocol-block structure cannot be fully excluded. To validate the SA's sensitivity to spurious structure, a \textbf{stricter negative control} was performed using \textbf{run-length-preserving block-level label shuffling}. This procedure permutes contiguous task blocks \textit{within each subject}, thereby preserving both the subject-specific class balance and the internal temporal structure of the labels while explicitly breaking feature-label associations. As shown in Table \ref{tab:wesad_sa}, the observed coherence $T_{obs}=0.71$ was higher than the block-shuffled null mean $\bar{T}_{null}=0.52$ under the SA test ($\hat{p}_{shuf}=0.001$), indicating statistically significant non-random organization in the tested ECG/EDA feature representation. The SA does not impose a cap. However, because $L_{PA}=2$, the weakened protocol-linked interpretation is reported with an interpretive Level 2 cap ($L_{Final} = 2$).

The ACC-only model includes 3-axis statistical features (mean, std, max) and SMA. The classifier baseline is used only to demonstrate the decoupling between predictive performance and claim scope; it is not used by LabelScope to assign the cap.

\subsection{SWELL-KW: Resolution Audit Case Study}
The SWELL-KW evaluation is intended as a primary case study for the Resolution Audit (RA). The initial minute-level claim on SWELL-KW resulted in $R_P = 15$, capping the claim at \textbf{Level 0}. Table \ref{tab:swell_redesign} illustrates how claim redesign can remove the RA-imposed cap. Table \ref{tab:swell_performance} details the performance inflation that occurs at higher resolutions. This RA outcome is unchanged across $\tau_{RA} = 2.0$--$10.0$; for $\tau_{RA} \ge 15.0$, the 1-min claim would not be capped by RA, highlighting the importance of setting $\tau_{RA}$ according to the desired replication safety margin. These values are not intended as a direct model-selection comparison across resolutions, because the aggregation unit and effective sample size change with the claim specification. Rather, the analysis shows that predictive performance is highly sensitive to the mismatch between the modeling unit and the independent label unit. This analysis is intended as an evaluation-unit sensitivity check rather than a classifier optimization experiment.

\section{Discussion: Implications for HMS Design}
\label{sec:discussion}

Table \ref{tab:reporting_comparison} illustrates how LabelScope changes the interpretation of a typical WESAD result. LabelScope addresses this interpretive gap by auditing whether the operational label can support the intended HMS claim. With the audit, the weakened protocol-linked interpretation is bounded at Level 2, conveying that the model likely tracks task-linked activity rather than movement-independent physiological state.

This proactive auditing may help reduce the risk of trust degradation by preventing misdirected interventions based on over-interpreted physiological proxies.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
While LabelScope provides a rigorous foundation, it is not a ``validity oracle.'' Its results are sensitive to the choice of features, neighborhood definitions, and audit thresholds. A sensitivity analysis (detailed in the supplementary archive) evaluated the stability of the WESAD PA outcome across a range of thresholds ($\tau_{PA} \in [0.2, 0.8]$); the resulting group-level Level 2 cap remained stable for $\tau_{PA} \le 0.6$, after which the group-mean rule no longer imposed a collective cap.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Absence of Closed-Loop Evaluation}: The current study evaluates LabelScope through retrospective audits. Determining how audit-triggered claim caps affect actual system behavior requires rigorous user studies in future deployments.
    \item \textbf{Epistemological Limits of the Proxy Audit}: The PA only audits against \textit{observed} covariates ($C_B$). A ``Level 3'' claim indicates only that no evidence of proxy risk was found under the tested configuration; it cannot guarantee the absence of unmeasured entanglements. 
    \item \textbf{SA and Claim Supportability}: In WESAD, SA checks for detectable organization in the tested ECG/EDA feature space; it does not rescue the claim from PA/RA-imposed caps. While the $\pm 60$\,s k-NN exclusion reduces immediate temporal adjacency artifacts, the ``Same-block exclusion'' in Table \ref{tab:wesad_sa} (where k-NN candidates are restricted to different subject-condition segments) provides a stricter check against protocol-block artifacts. Future work should evaluate even stricter controls, such as condition-held-out SA and block-identity-balanced negative controls.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
We have presented LabelScope, a conservative framework for auditing state-label validity in physiology-based HMS. By shifting the focus from predictive accuracy to claim supportability, LabelScope supports more cautious and reproducible reporting. As HMS are deployed in increasingly consequential environments, systematic auditing of claim supportability will be an important step toward building human-centered systems with a well-characterized scope of inference.



\section*{Data and Code Availability}
The WESAD and SWELL-KW datasets are publicly available. The supplementary archive contains the preprocessing configuration (JSON), LOSO prediction records (CSV), bootstrap scripts, SA negative-control implementations (Python), cross-subject and condition-exclusion SA scripts, WESAD subject-wise PA distribution tables, SWELL-KW aggregation scripts, random seeds, and automated table-generation code to support peer review and full reproducibility. The implementation-level physiological feature definitions and behavioral covariate details are also included in the supplementary material to ensure a compact and focused main manuscript. LabelScope is designed to be reproducible under fixed preprocessing parameters and permutation seeds. The implementation uses NeuroKit2 (v0.2.1) and cvxEDA.
